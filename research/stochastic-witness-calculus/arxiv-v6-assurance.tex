\section{Certified Computation and Distributional Reliability}\label{sec:assurance}

The practical use of \SWC{} does not require settling an infinite existence
conjecture.  A system can instead certify its returned answers, prove coverage
of a declared finite domain, and measure verified-answer availability under a
specified input distribution.  Each conclusion has a distinct evidence record.
The following contracts operationalize the exact and empirical modes already
defined in this paper; their logical and statistical ingredients are classical.

\subsection{Exact outputs and complete finite coverage}

\begin{theorem}[Certified-output contract]\label{thm:output-contract}
Let $V(x,w)$ be a deterministic verifier with proved soundness
$V(x,w)=1\Rightarrow R(x,w)$. Suppose a wrapper emits an answer $w$ only after
$V(x,w)=1$, and otherwise reports unresolved. Every emitted answer satisfies
$R(x,w)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
An emitted answer satisfies the acceptance guard. Apply verifier soundness.
The statement requires neither termination nor successful search on every input.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Finite operating-domain contract]\label{thm:finite-contract}
Let a manifest define a nonempty finite domain $D$ independently of the
producer's successes. If a checker reconstructs $D$, verifies one sound
witness for each member, and checks that no member is omitted, then
$\forall x\in D\;\exists w\;R(x,w)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For arbitrary $x\in D$, completeness of the manifest traversal supplies its
accepted witness. Apply soundness. The conclusion is restricted to $D$.
\end{proof}

The implementation binds each receipt to its relation, input, witness, and
single-instance scope. The domain checker reconstructs the specified
combinations and rejects omitted, duplicate, or mismatched rows. Fingerprints
identify the checked bytes; they do not replace verification or authenticate
the person who supplied the data. The exact Python implementation remains
part of the trusted computing base.

\subsection{Fixed-horizon availability guarantee}

Freeze a solver configuration and an input distribution $P$. Draw
$X_1,\ldots,X_N$ independently from $P$, and set $F_i=1$ whenever no verified
receipt is returned. This includes unresolved searches, invalid candidates,
errors, and timeouts. Let $p=\Pr_P(F_i=1)$ and $f=\sum_iF_i$.

\begin{proposition}[Zero-failure reliability contract]\label{prop:reliability}
Choose $N$, $\epsilon\in(0,1)$, and $\alpha\in(0,1)$ before examining outcomes,
and suppose $(1-\epsilon)^N\le\alpha$. Under the stated IID model, a protocol
that declares the target $p\le\epsilon$ only after observing zero failures has
false-certification probability at most $\alpha$ when $p>\epsilon$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For $p>\epsilon$, the probability of zero failures is
$(1-p)^N\le(1-\epsilon)^N\le\alpha$.
\end{proof}

This is the zero-failure specialization of a one-sided exact binomial
confidence bound. More generally, inversion of the binomial lower tail yields
a Clopper--Pearson upper bound. The implementation uses rational arithmetic
and conservative rounding. It also verifies a chosen rational target directly
against the exact tail probability. See the NIST exact-binomial
documentation\footnote{\url{https://itl.nist.gov/div898/software/dataplot/refman2/auxillar/exacbino.htm}}.
This fixed-horizon contract does not permit unaccounted optional stopping.
The time-uniform methods discussed earlier provide a different protocol with
their own assumptions \cite{HowardEtAl2021}.

\subsection{Recorded operating-domain and fresh-input evaluation}

For this demonstration, the relation is the stationary Lonely Runner condition
for seven distinct positive speeds, with threshold $1/8$. The finite domain is
\[
 D=\left\{V\subseteq\{1,\ldots,20\}: |V|=7,\ \gcd(V)=1,
   \ \forall r\in\{2,\ldots,8\}\ \exists v\in V:\ r\mid v\right\}.
\]
The main checker and an independent implementation enumerated all $12{,}673$
members and checked a rational witness for each. This establishes complete
coverage of that domain; no new general Lonely Runner theorem is claimed.

A separate plan was recorded before sampling. It froze the solver, selected
$N=4{,}603$, and prescribed uniform seven-element subsets of $\{1,\ldots,60\}$
with replacement between trials. Each input tuple contains distinct speeds;
there is no rejection filter for openness, primitiveness, difficulty, or
success. Python's \texttt{SystemRandom} supplied operating-system randomness,
whose IID sampling behavior is an explicit modeling assumption.

statement concerns the failure probability of this frozen solver under the
declared distribution, not the truth of an individual verified witness and not
an arbitrary deployment population. The raw ledger includes every trial, with
plan, configuration, and receipt fingerprints. The independent verifier checked
all receipts and recomputed the binomial threshold.

\subsection{A failed canonical restriction as a support regression}

The search interface attempts cheap canonical pair-position proposals before
a complete pair-sum fallback. The need for that fallback is demonstrated by
the primitive open speed set $(44,60,104,205)$, with threshold $1/5$.
Two proposed finite canonical target lists both fail on this set, even when
non-unit multipliers are permitted. Nevertheless $t=9/148$ has distances
\[
  \left(\frac{12}{37},\frac{13}{37},\frac{12}{37},\frac{69}{148}\right),
\]
whose minimum exceeds $1/5$. An independent checker enumerated every
multiplier for every pair, checking $1{,}239$ seeds with $212$ accepted seeds.
The full lists and negative canonical certificate are in the supplement.
This refutes the proposed canonical restriction, not Lonely Runner.

The complete pair-sum candidate support uses the established local-maximum
result of Kravitz\footnote{N. Kravitz, \emph{Barely lonely runners and very
lonely runners}, Combinatorial Theory 1 (2021), Proposition 2.1.
\url{https://escholarship.org/uc/item/3wx931fh}}. A canonical miss is therefore
unresolved until the complete fallback is executed. If its work budget is
exceeded, the interface returns unresolved rather than inferring nonexistence.

An atomic candidate distribution also retains boundary witnesses that a
continuous proposal can miss with probability one. For example, for speeds
$(1,2)$ the times satisfying threshold $1/3$ in $[0,1)$ are exactly
$\{1/3,2/3\}$. This accepted set has Lebesgue measure zero but mass $2/3$
under the uniform pair-sum grid. This is an application of support choice,
not a proof that every instance has positive accepted mass.

\subsection{Measurement and reproducibility scope}

For mathematical inputs, the ground-truth relation is the exact predicate
being checked. For a physical application, the record must additionally state
measurement provenance, calibration, uncertainty, the relevant population,
and a success definition. Exact calculation over recorded values establishes
a fact about those values; claims about a physical system require measurement
and sampling assumptions. No physical deployment was evaluated here.

The supplement includes the frozen plan, every finite-domain witness, the
complete fresh-trial ledger, and independent verification commands. Fifty-seven
test methods pass across the exact kernel, canonical audits, and operating
contracts. Tests support the implementation but are not proof-assistant
verification or independent peer review. The earlier artifact's checksum
reconciliation remains a separate publication task.
